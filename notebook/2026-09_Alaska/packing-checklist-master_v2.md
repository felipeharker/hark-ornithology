# Alaskan Cruise Gear List

**Trip:** round-trip Seattle, Inside Passage, early September 2026 (~Sept 1–12)
**Ports:** Ketchikan · Juneau · Skagway · Glacier Bay / Endicott Arm (scenic cruising)
· Sitka and/or Victoria BC · land days in Seattle both ends
**Booked excursions:** whale watching (small boat) · White Pass railway / Yukon
· Mendenhall and rainforest hiking

Companion notes: [`birding-targets.md`](birding-targets.md) (what to look for)
and [`birding-by-port.md`](birding-by-port.md) (where to look for it).

---

## Conditions this list is built for

| Place | Avg high | Avg low | Sept precip | Wet days |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Ketchikan | 59 °F / 15 °C | 49 °F / 9 °C | ~17.7 in | ~22 |
| Juneau | 52 °F / 11 °C | 43 °F / 6 °C | ~14 in | ~19 |
| Skagway | 54 °F / 12 °C | 43 °F / 6 °C | ~3.1 in | ~19 |
| Seattle / Victoria | low 70s °F | mid 50s °F | ~1.5 in | ~9 |

Four things drive every clothing decision below:

- **September is the wettest month of the year in Southeast Alaska.** Juneau
  averages 14 in over 19 days; Ketchikan is the rain capital of the state at
  ~17.7 in. Assume rain on most port days rather than hoping otherwise.
- **Skagway sits in a rain shadow** — barely a fifth of Ketchikan's rainfall.
  It will likely be the one dry, bright port. Plan the long walks there.
- **It is never warm, and it is never truly cold.** The whole trip lives in the
  40s and 50s °F. The enemy is wet plus wind, not low temperature.
- **Two places run far colder than the port averages:** the White Pass summit
  (~2,865 ft, routinely 35–45 °F with wind, September snow possible) and the
  open deck in Glacier Bay, where katabatic wind pouring off the ice makes a
  45 °F morning feel near freezing. These two set the warm end of the kit.

Daylight is generous but shrinking: Juneau runs ~14 h on Sept 1 (sunrise 5:56,
sunset 19:59) down to ~13 h by Sept 12. Juneau averages only 6.7 h of
clear or sunny sky in September — plan on flat, dim, grey light.

## Clothing

Nothing cotton, anywhere in this section. Cotton holds water, stops insulating
the moment it is damp, and will not dry overnight in a humid cabin. That rules
out jeans, cotton tees, and cotton socks — the three things most people default
to packing.

### The system

Four layers, mixed and matched per outing rather than one heavy coat:

1. **Base** — next to skin, wicking
2. **Mid** — breathable warmth you hike in
3. **Insulation** — standing-still warmth, worn only when stopped
4. **Shell** — fully waterproof, worn far more often than you expect

### Base layers

- Merino or synthetic long-sleeve crew, ~150–200 g/m² — 3
- Merino or synthetic T-shirt — 2–3
- Long underwear bottoms, lightweight — 1–2 (Glacier Bay deck, White Pass)
- Merino boxers/briefs — 1 per day, they pack tiny

### Mid layers

- Grid fleece pullover or ¼-zip (Polartec Power Grid or similar) — 2.
  Grid fleece breathes while you climb and still holds warmth wet, which a
  smooth-face fleece does not.
- Light wool sweater or second fleece for evenings — 1

### Insulation

- **Synthetic-fill puffy jacket (PrimaLoft or similar) — 1.** Deliberately not
  down. Down collapses when wet and takes days to loft again; in a place
  averaging 17 in of rain a month, synthetic keeps working damp. This is the
  single most-often-wrong item people bring to Southeast Alaska.
- Optional light down vest for under the shell on the glacier day, if you own one

### Shell — the most important item on this list

- **Hardshell rain jacket, fully waterproof with taped seams** (≥ 20,000 mm
  rating). Not a softshell, not a "water-resistant" DWR wind jacket, not a
  packable emergency poncho. Specifics that matter:
  - hood with a **stiffened brim** and room for a beanie underneath
  - pit zips — you will overheat hiking Mendenhall in the rain
  - long enough in the back to cover you sitting on a wet boat bench
  - large enough to close over the puffy
- **Rain pants — full-length side zips.** The zips let you get them on over
  boots on a rocking boat deck, which is exactly when you will need them.
  Skipping rain pants is the other classic mistake: the whale-watch boat, the
  Mendenhall trail, and a Ketchikan afternoon all soak you from the knees down.
- Packable poncho or a large trash bag — costs nothing, covers the camera bag

### Legs

- Quick-dry nylon hiking pants — 2
- Softshell or fleece-lined pants for the cold days — 1
- Comfortable pants for onboard/evening — 1–2
- **No jeans.** They will get wet on day one and stay wet all week.

### Extremities

- **Thin liner gloves you can work camera dials in** — 2 pairs, so one can dry.
  Test them on the 5Ds before you go: if you cannot change the shutter dial
  wearing them, they are the wrong gloves.
- Waterproof shell gloves or mitts to go over the liners — 1 pair
- Warm beanie that fits under the shell hood — 1
- **Brimmed hat or cap — 1.** Not vanity: a brim keeps rain off your binocular
  oculars and the camera viewfinder, which is the difference between birding in
  the rain and giving up.
- Buff / neck gaiter — 1–2. Cheap, tiny, and the best wind defence on an open
  boat deck.

### Footwear

- **Waterproof hiking boots with real lugged tread** — Mendenhall and rainforest
  trails are wet roots, mud, and slick rock. Ankle support helps on both.
- **Rubber knee boots (Xtratufs or equivalent)** — the actual Southeast Alaska
  standard footwear. Perfect for wet boat decks, the tide flats at Dyea, and
  shoreline birding where the good shorebirds are below the tideline. If you
  only bring one wet-weather pair, the local answer is these.
- Comfortable shoes for the ship and evenings
- **Wool socks — 1 pair per day, minimum.** Boots do not dry overnight in a
  humid cabin; dry socks are how you cope. Two pairs of thicker ones for the
  glacier and pass days.
- Gaiters, if you own them — worth it on the Mendenhall trails

### Onboard and evening

`planning.md` has dinners on board as an open item — check the line's dress
code and pack to it.

- Smart-casual dinner outfits — 2–3
- One dressier option if the sailing has a formal night
- Swimsuit (ship hot tubs, and they are genuinely good in the rain)
- Sleepwear, and something to cross the hall in

### What to wear when

| Situation | Layers |
| --- | --- |
| Glacier Bay / Endicott Arm deck, standing 2+ h | base + grid fleece + puffy + shell + rain pants + beanie + shell gloves + buff |
| Whale-watch small boat | base + fleece + shell + rain pants + rubber boots + buff; puffy in the bag |
| White Pass rail or Yukon bus | base + long johns + fleece + puffy + shell; open platforms are cold and windy |
| Mendenhall / rainforest hike | base + light fleece + shell; leave the puffy in the pack or you will soak it with sweat |
| Ketchikan town day | base + shell + rain pants + rubber boots; assume rain |
| Skagway town walk | base + fleece + shell; likely the driest day of the trip |
| Seattle / Victoria | base + one light layer; the puffy stays in the bag |

## Photography

### Body and glass

- Canon EOS 5Ds
- 50 mm EF lens
- 150–600 mm EF lens
- Lens filters, adapter etc. — a clear/UV filter on the 150–600 is worth it
  here purely as a rain and salt-spray shield on the front element

### Cards and storage

- **CompactFlash cards (UDMA 7) — the gap in the current list.** The 5Ds has
  two slots: CF Type I and SD (UHS-I). The list only covers SD, which leaves
  half the camera unused and the buffer slower than it needs to be on a bird
  in flight. Bring CF, or make a deliberate decision to shoot SD-only.
- 64–128 GB SD cards, multiple, blank — as listed
- SD to USB-C adapter
- **CF card reader with USB-C** — if you bring CF cards, the iPad cannot ingest
  them without one
- 1 TB SSD with USB-C

  Capacity math: 50.6 MP RAW files run ~60–70 MB. A 64 GB card holds roughly
  900–1,000 frames — about one active day. The 1 TB SSD is right-sized for the
  trip, but plan on ~8 GB per 120 frames when you decide how many cards to buy.

### Power

- LP-E6 camera batteries — bring **4 or more**. Cold drains them fast; standing
  on deck for two hours in Glacier Bay will eat a battery faster than you expect.
  If you are buying, LP-E6N/LP-E6NH hold more than the original LP-E6.
- **Two** LP-E6 chargers with USB-C — so four batteries turn around overnight
- Keep spares in an inside jacket pocket, not the camera bag; body heat keeps
  their capacity up

### Weather protection — the real gap for this trip

- **Proper rain cover for the 5Ds + 150–600** (Think Tank Hydrophobia,
  LensCoat RainCoat or similar). A supermarket bag and a rubber band is not a
  plan for a month averaging 17 in of rain, and the 5Ds is not a weather-sealed
  body in the way that phrase is often used.
- Lens wipes, spray, microfibre towels — as listed. Add one **chamois or
  waffle-weave towel**: microfibre smears on genuinely wet glass.
- Rocket blower and a soft brush — salt spray dries to a crust
- Large zip-lock bags and silica gel packs. **Seal the camera in a bag before
  carrying it from cold outside into the warm cabin** — otherwise condensation
  forms on and inside the lens and you lose an hour waiting it out.
- Spare body cap and rear lens cap; gaffer tape

### Support

- **Monopod, not a tripod.** Six hundred millimetres handheld on a moving deck
  for hours is punishing, but a tripod is useless on a ship — it transmits hull
  vibration straight into the frame. A monopod takes the weight and lets the
  lens float. Check the cruise line's policy first; some restrict tripods and
  monopods in public areas.

### Settings worth deciding before you go

- The 5Ds has no in-body stabilisation — leave the lens's OS/VC on, and use its
  panning mode for birds in flight.
- Juneau gets 6.7 h of clear sky in September. Plan to accept ISO 3200 and a
  fast shutter rather than a clean file that is motion-blurred; at 50 MP, camera
  shake shows up ruthlessly.
- Leave the teleconverter at home if you own one — 600 mm at f/6.3 plus 1.4× is
  f/9, and the 5Ds's autofocus is not happy there.

## General Electronics

- iPad Pro — for nightly culling and as the second backup copy
- Multi-charge power bank — **carry-on only**; airlines require lithium banks in
  the cabin, under 100 Wh
- USB-C to USB-C cables
- USB-C to AC adapter blocks
- **Non-surge-protected multi-outlet cube.** Cabins have very few outlets. Note
  that most cruise lines confiscate power strips and extension cords that
  include surge protection — bring a plain USB hub/cube instead.
- Headlamp with a red mode — early departures, pre-dawn deck time
- Wall clock/alarm or a charged phone; ship time can differ from phone time
- Download everything before sailing. Onboard wifi is slow and expensive.

## Birding

- **Binoculars/optics** — 8× is the better choice over 10× for this trip: most
  of your birding is from a moving deck or a small boat, and 10× magnifies the
  motion as much as the bird.
- **Binocular harness** rather than a neck strap. A full day carrying both
  binoculars and a 150–600 makes this non-optional.
- Rain guard for the binocular oculars, and objective covers
- Lens pen or dedicated cloth kept dry inside a pocket for the binoculars
- **Spotting scope — optional, and I would leave it** unless you already own a
  compact one with a light tripod. It is dead weight on the ship, but it would
  genuinely pay off at Mendenhall Wetlands, Eagle Beach, and the Ogden Point
  breakwater in Victoria, where the birds sit far out on the flats or the water.
- **Waterproof field notebook (Rite in the Rain or similar) and a pencil or
  all-weather pen.** An ordinary notebook and a ballpoint will fail on the first
  wet day — this is the one place to spend $8 and save a trip's worth of notes.

- Passport, Brazil

## Health and Comfort

- **Motion sickness remedy — take it before boarding the small boat, not after.**
  The whale-watch tender and any open-water stretch (Queen Charlotte Sound is
  genuinely exposed) will find out whether you get seasick. Meclizine, ginger
  chews, or a scopolamine patch if your doctor agrees.
- Blister care — moleskin or hydrocolloid patches
- Ibuprofen, antihistamine, any personal prescriptions
- Sunscreen and lip balm — glare off water and ice burns even under cloud
- Insect repellent — early September is past the worst, but Mendenhall still has
  them
- Reusable water bottle, and a thermos worth filling before a cold deck morning
- Earplugs and an eye mask
- Hand warmers — a few disposable packs for the Glacier Bay deck

**Bear note:** hiking near salmon streams in September means bears actively
feeding on the run. Stay in groups, make noise, keep distance, and never
position yourself between a bear and the water for a photograph.
