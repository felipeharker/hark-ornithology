# Birding by Port — Inside Passage, Early September 2026

Where to actually put yourself. Companion to
[`birding-targets.md`](birding-targets.md) (what to look for) and
[`packing-checklist-master_v2.md`](packing-checklist-master_v2.md).

A port call gives you roughly eight to nine hours, and a booked excursion eats
five to six of them. Assume **one** birding decision per port, not three.

---

## From the ship

The sailing itself is the largest block of birding time on the trip, and the
only way you will see open-water species. Treat it as a site.

**Where to stand.** The aft deck is the best position: it gives shelter from
the apparent wind, and gulls, kittiwakes and jaegers follow the wake. A
forward-facing promenade deck is better for birds you overtake — murrelets and
guillemots sitting on the water ahead, which flush as the hull approaches.
Work both.

**When.** First light and the hour before dusk are the productive windows, and
in early September that means roughly 06:00 and 19:30. This is also when the
ship is usually threading the narrowest, most sheltered water, which is where
the birds are.

**Which water matters.** Inside Passage channels — Chatham Strait, Stephens
Passage, Lynn Canal — are protected and hold Marbled Murrelet, Pigeon Guillemot,
Harlequin Duck, Surf and White-winged Scoter, Bald Eagle throughout. The
exposed crossings are the ones to be on deck for: **Queen Charlotte Sound** and
**Dixon Entrance** are open ocean, and they are where jaegers, shearwaters,
Fork-tailed Storm-Petrel and the odd albatross become possible. Find out when
the ship crosses them and set an alarm.

**Logging it.** A single checklist spanning 200 miles of ocean is not useful
data. Break deck sessions into short traveling counts — an hour or so each,
noting start position — or log genuinely notable birds as incidental. Record
the ship's position from the cabin TV or the navigation channel when you start.

**Photography from a moving ship:** monopod, never a tripod, and expect to work
at high ISO in flat light. See the photography notes in the packing list.

## Glacier Bay or Endicott Arm — the big day

The single best birding day of the trip, and the one none of your field guides
covers. Full deck day, no excursion competing for your time.

**Targets:** Kittlitz's Murrelet (this is the reason to be out there), Horned
and Tufted Puffin, Black-legged Kittiwake in numbers, Common Murre, Marbled
Murrelet, Pigeon Guillemot, Black Oystercatcher on rocky edges, Harlequin Duck
along the shoreline, Arctic Tern, Bald Eagle throughout.

**Where to look.** Kittlitz's separates from Marbled by habitat as much as by
plumage: it favours the turbid, silty grey water near tidewater glacier faces,
where Marbled tends to prefer clearer water. Scan the meltwater plume, not the
open channel. You will get seconds per bird — study the plates the night before.

**Dress for it properly.** Katabatic wind pours off the ice and makes a 45 °F
morning feel near freezing. This is the day the synthetic puffy, shell gloves,
beanie and buff all come out at once.

**Find the ranger.** Most Glacier Bay itineraries take National Park Service
rangers aboard for the day. They are the best local knowledge available to you
all week, and they generally welcome someone who actually wants to talk about
birds. Get to them early, before the crowds.

## Ketchikan

Assume rain — ~17.7 in falls here in September across ~22 days, and it is the
wettest port on the route. Plan a wet day and be pleasantly surprised.

**In town, no transport needed.** **Ketchikan Creek** runs straight through the
middle of town at Creek Street. In early September it is full of spawning
salmon, which concentrates Bald Eagles, gulls and ravens; watch the fast water
above the falls for **American Dipper**. Fifteen minutes from the ship.

**Walkable.** A separated path runs along **Tongass Highway** south toward
Saxman — a couple of flat miles along the water, good for scanning the channel
for Marbled Murrelet, Pigeon Guillemot, Pelagic Cormorant, Harlequin Duck and
the rocky-shore shorebirds. Scan every rock groyne and breakwater for **Black
Turnstone** and **Surfbird**.

**Worth a taxi (~7 miles north).** **Ward Lake Recreation Area** has a flat
1.3-mile loop through spruce–hemlock — the best mature-forest birding of the
port, with Red-breasted Sapsucker, Townsend's Warbler, Pacific Wren,
Chestnut-backed Chickadee. It is also an owl site: Barred, Northern Saw-whet
and Northern Pygmy-Owl have all been recorded, and Western Screech-Owl resides
around Signal Creek Campground. Barrow's Goldeneye and Hooded Merganser use
the lake, though early September is early for them.

**Probably too far:** Settlers Cove State Recreation Site is 16 miles north at
the end of the road — excellent for Harlequin Duck, scoters, grebes and
murrelets over Clover Passage, but it needs most of a port day.

## Juneau

Your Mendenhall excursion covers the glacier and rainforest. Use it well, then
decide whether the wetlands are worth the rest of the day.

**On the excursion — Steep Creek.** The boardwalk beside the Mendenhall Glacier
Visitor Center follows Steep Creek, where **American Dipper** breeds and the
nearby ponds hold Harlequin Duck and Common Merganser. In early September the
creek has spawning salmon and, reliably, black bears feeding on them. This is
probably your best dipper opportunity of the trip and it is on your itinerary
already. Bald Eagles perch on the icebergs in the lake.

**The rainforest trails** off the visitor centre — Trail of Time, Moraine
Ecology Trail, Nugget Falls — give you the mature forest passerines: Varied
Thrush, Pacific Wren, Chestnut-backed Chickadee, Golden-crowned Kinglet,
Red-breasted Sapsucker, Townsend's and Wilson's Warbler while they are still
moving through.

**If you have time left — Mendenhall Wetlands State Game Refuge.** Four
thousand acres of tidal flats, the region's best shorebird and waterfowl site,
and an Important Bird Area. Southbound shorebirds run from late July, and
waterfowl arrive through late August and September, so early September catches
both. The two access points that matter:

- **Fish Creek, Douglas Island** — reachable by city bus to Douglas
- **Radcliffe Road, at the west end of the airport**

Bring a scope if you brought one; the birds are far out on the flats. Time the
tide — a rising tide pushes shorebirds toward you, dead low scatters them to
the horizon.

**Eagle Beach State Recreation Area** (~28 miles north) is the other classic
Juneau site, but it is a long way for a port day.

## Skagway

Likely your one dry, bright port — Skagway sits in a rain shadow and averages
~3.1 in in September against Ketchikan's ~17.7. Spend the walking energy here.

**The White Pass excursion is your alpine day.** The railway and the highway
both climb to around 2,865 ft and top out above treeline, which puts you in
ptarmigan and Spruce Grouse habitat without a hike. Watch the tundra and the
rock edges from the platform:

- **Rock Ptarmigan** (C on both the Juneau and Skagway lists), **Willow
  Ptarmigan** (FC), **White-tailed Ptarmigan** (U) — still in brown plumage in
  early September, so look for movement, not white
- **Spruce Grouse** — C on the Skagway list and effectively absent from the
  other two; the White Pass road corridor is your one real shot
- **Sooty Grouse** in the subalpine below the pass
- **Gray-crowned Rosy-Finch** and **American Pipit** on the alpine scree
- **Golden Eagle** over the ridges; **Pine Grosbeak** (C at Skagway) in the
  conifers

Dress for near-freezing with wind. The open rail platforms are cold and the
weather at the summit is not the weather in town.

**Walkable from the dock.** **Yakutania Point** — cross the footbridge over
the Skagway River at the southwest corner of town, then half a mile of trail.
Scan Taiya Inlet and the river mouth for scoters, Barrow's Goldeneye,
Bufflehead, Harlequin Duck and Red-breasted Merganser. Arctic Terns nest near
the footbridge, though early September is late for them.

**Gold Rush Cemetery and Lower Reid Falls** — about 2.5 flat miles from the
dock. The falls are another good dipper site, and the walk out passes decent
riparian cover.

**Dyea Flats** (~9 miles, needs a taxi) is the Taiya River estuary — great blue
herons year-round, plus shorebirds and waterfowl on the estuary. Worth it only
if White Pass is cancelled for weather.

## Sitka, if called

The one port on the outer coast rather than the Inside Passage, which changes
the species list — this is where genuine open-ocean birds come within reach.
**St. Lazaria Island**, a seabird colony in Sitka Sound, is the local draw
(puffins, murres, storm-petrels), but it requires a boat tour; check whether one
fits the port hours. Onshore, **Starrigavan Recreation Area** at the north end
of the road has an estuary boardwalk and forest trails.

## Victoria BC, if called

Usually a short evening call, which limits you to whatever is close and
whatever light remains. Two sites are built for exactly this:

- **Clover Point** — faces the Strait of Juan de Fuca, and you can bird it
  from a parked car, which matters in weather
- **Ogden Point breakwater** — a proper platform for scoping deep-water
  seabirds, and the closest thing to a pelagic you can walk to

Early September is good for shorebirds on the southern Vancouver Island coast.

## Seattle land days

Your only non-Alaska birding, and worth planning rather than leaving to chance —
early September in Puget Sound is genuinely good, and everything here is new to
you except the seven species from Salmon Creek.

**Discovery Park** — 534 acres on Magnolia Bluff, over 270 species recorded, and
the premier birding site in the city. The bluff concentrates migrant passerines
moving down the Puget Sound corridor. In fall, watch offshore for **Parasitic
Jaegers** harassing Bonaparte's Gulls, and work the rocky beach for
**Surfbird**, **Black Turnstone** and **Ruddy Turnstone**.

**Union Bay Natural Area (Montlake Fill)** — ~200 species, and the ponds are the
best freshwater shorebird habitat in the city in September: Pectoral, Baird's,
Semipalmated, Solitary and Stilt Sandpiper, Wilson's Phalarope.

**Alki Beach, West Seattle** — flat shoreline walking with Puget Sound scanning.

Do the marine sites at a rising tide, and the Fill early in the morning.
