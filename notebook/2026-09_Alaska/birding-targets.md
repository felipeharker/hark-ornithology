# Target Species — Southeast Alaska, Early September 2026

Companion to [`packing-checklist-master_v2.md`](packing-checklist-master_v2.md)

## The headline

Your eBird life list stands at **151 species**, built almost entirely in Texas
and Rio de Janeiro, plus seven records from one December morning in Clark
County, Washington. Essentially the whole Pacific temperate rainforest and
North Pacific coastal avifauna is new to you.

Against the three regional checklists in `field-guides/`, filtered to early
September and cross-referenced with your eBird export:

| Abundance in early Sept | New to you | What it means |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Common | **59** | You should get most of these without trying hard |
| Fairly common | **18** | Likely, given time in the right habitat |
| Uncommon | **43** | Available with deliberate effort at the right site |
| Rare but annual | **42** | Luck, or a very good day |

A realistic target is **70–90 lifers**, and a hard-working trip with decent
weather could pass 100.

## How this list was built

Parsed from the PDFs in `field-guides/` and cross-referenced against
`observation-data/ebird-data-latest.csv`:

| Source | Coverage | Season column used |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `alaska-juneau-2007.pdf` | 282 species; Juneau road/trail system and adjacent marine waters | **Su** = Jun 22 – Sep 21 |
| `alaska-ketchikan-2009.pdf` | 264 species; Ketchikan Gateway Borough | **F** = Aug – Nov |
| `alaska-skagway-.pdf` | Skagway Bird Club and NPS coastal waterbird survey | **S** and **F** |

`alaska-general-2026.pdf` is the statewide Alaska Bird Records Committee list —
good for judging the status of a rarity, useless for planning a day.
`alaska-western-2026.pdf` covers Bird Conservation Region 102, which is Nome and
the Bering Sea coast, and has no bearing on an Inside Passage sailing. The
`british columbia` and `washington` files are Avibase region lists with no
seasonal abundance, so they inform the Victoria and Seattle days as
presence/absence only.

Codes: **C** common · **FC** fairly common · **U** uncommon · **R** rare ·
**V** very rare or casual · **—** no records, or the species is not on that
list. **◇** marks species the Juneau list flags as irruptive — crossbills,
siskins, redpolls — which swing wildly year to year and cannot be predicted.
Where a checklist gave codes only on subspecies rows (Northern Flicker,
Yellow-rumped Warbler, Dark-eyed Junco), the best subspecies code is used.

### The caveat that matters most

**The three checklists disagree about what "September" means, and none of the
columns is centred on your dates.** Juneau's "Summer" runs June 22 – Sept 21,
so it is weighted toward midsummer. Ketchikan's "Fall" runs August – November,
so it is weighted toward late autumn. Early September falls in the gap — which
is useful, because it tells you which way each column is lying.

**Species the Ketchikan column flatters.** Late-fall arrivals whose strong
rating comes from October and November. Ketchikan's own *summer* code for each
is V or R, so in the first ten days of September you are early — expect a few
birds, not the rafts:

> Barrow's Goldeneye · Bufflehead · Long-tailed Duck · Common Goldeneye ·
> Pacific Loon · Red-throated Loon · Red-necked Grebe · Pelagic Cormorant ·
> Double-crested Cormorant

**Species the Juneau column flatters.** Breeders already leaving. Two are
effectively gone by your dates — **Rufous Hummingbird** (adults leave in July;
Ketchikan rates it R in fall) and **Arctic Tern** — and these are in their last
window:

> Lesser Yellowlegs · Short-billed Dowitcher · Pacific-slope Flycatcher ·
> Swainson's Thrush · Barn Swallow · Western and Least Sandpiper ·
> Yellow, Wilson's, Townsend's and Orange-crowned Warbler

**The safest bets are the species that hold up in every column** — the resident
rainforest and marine birds marked C across all three lists below.

## Timing: what early September buys you

**Gains.** You catch the tail of southbound shorebird passage; juveniles are
still moving through Mendenhall Wetlands and the Ketchikan flats through the
first half of the month, and juvenile plumage on peeps and dowitchers is
generally easier to work than worn adult plumage. The late warblers, the
flycatchers and the thrushes are still present. Waterfowl are beginning to
build. Weather is the best odds September offers, and daylight is still
13–14 hours.

**Losses.** Rufous Hummingbird is realistically gone and Arctic Terns have
mostly left. Swallow numbers are collapsing. You are too early for the late
September and October seaduck spectacle — the big scoter, goldeneye and
Long-tailed Duck rafts — and for the wintering alcid and loon build-up.

**Not on any of these checklists, but in season:** thousands of **Sandhill
Cranes** stage through Southeast Alaska on migration, peaking in September. All
three port lists rate them R or V because the staging happens outside those
checklist areas — but a flock going over is hard to miss, so keep an ear up.

## The prizes

Ranked by what would actually make the trip, not by rarity alone.

**Kittlitz's Murrelet.** Glacier Bay holds a large share of the world
population, and a cruise ship is one of the few practical ways to see one. Look
in the turbid, silty water near tidewater glacier faces, which is where it
separates from Marbled Murrelet. Worth knowing the ethical wrinkle: research in
Glacier Bay found roughly 61% of murrelets within 850 m of a cruise ship are
disturbed into flushing or diving. You are a passenger, not the pilot, but it
is part of the picture.

**The ptarmigan trio.** Rock Ptarmigan (C on the Juneau and Skagway lists),
Willow Ptarmigan (FC) and White-tailed Ptarmigan (U) are all in play, and the
White Pass corridor puts you in alpine habitat without a climb. In early
September they are still mostly in brown plumage — harder to spot, much better
to photograph.

**Spruce Grouse.** C on the Skagway list and absent from the other two. The
White Pass road corridor is your one real shot.

**American Dipper.** C or FC on all three lists, and one of the most enjoyable
birds in the region. Any fast clear creek: Reid Falls in Skagway, Steep Creek at
Mendenhall, Ketchikan Creek in town.

**The rocky-shoreline trio.** Black Turnstone (C), Surfbird (FC) and Rock
Sandpiper (U) work exposed rock together. Scan every breakwater and groyne.

**Harlequin Duck.** C, on rocky coastline everywhere. Drakes in early September
are in eclipse and far duller than the field-guide plate — do not dismiss a
plain brown duck sitting on a rock.

**Marbled Murrelet.** Southeast Alaska holds the highest density in the world
and it is C on all three lists. Near-guaranteed, and a genuinely special bird —
it nests on mossy limbs high in old-growth conifers.

**Black Oystercatcher** (U), **Gray-crowned Rosy-Finch** (U, alpine at White
Pass), **Pine Grosbeak** (C at Skagway), **Varied Thrush** (C everywhere),
**Golden-crowned Sparrow** and **Northern Saw-whet Owl** round out the birds
worth going out of your way for.

## ID traps for early September

Almost everything will be in worn adult or fresh juvenile plumage, not the
breeding plumage the field guides lead with.

- **Gulls.** Glaucous-winged is the default; Herring, Short-billed (the former
  Mew Gull), California and Iceland (Thayer's) all occur. Glaucous-winged
  hybridises freely with Herring and Western and a large share of birds here are
  intergrades. Photograph the open wing and the bare parts and sort it out
  later — do not try to nail every bird in the field.
- **Dowitchers.** Both species occur. In early September most juveniles on the
  coast are Short-billed; call is the reliable separator, so record it.
- **Peeps.** Western, Least, Semipalmated and Baird's all appear. Leg colour
  (yellow on Least, black on the others) and bill shape do most of the work.
- **Murrelets.** Marbled in basic plumage looks nothing like the summer bird.
  Kittlitz's is paler-faced with a much shorter bill — study the plates before
  Glacier Bay, because you will get seconds, not minutes.
- **Scoters.** Surf and White-winged are both C; females and immatures are the
  hard ones. White-winged shows the wing patch when it flaps.
- **Empidonax.** Alder, Hammond's and Pacific-slope all occur and all are silent
  in September. Let the silent ones go.
- **Cormorants.** Pelagic (small, thin-necked), Double-crested, Brandt's. Size
  and neck thickness beat face colour at range.
- **Junco and crow.** Dark-eyed Junco here is the "Oregon" type, not the
  slate-coloured bird you know from Texas. The crow is the form formerly split
  as Northwestern Crow, now lumped into American Crow — so no tick, but it looks
  and sounds different.

## The gap: nothing here covers Glacier Bay

None of the Alaska PDFs covers Glacier Bay or Endicott Arm. The Juneau checklist
area stops at Berners Bay, which is why Kittlitz's Murrelet shows as very rare
there and Tufted Puffin as accidental — that reflects the checklist boundary,
not the glacier day. From a ship in Glacier Bay the National Park Service and
published trip accounts put the following in play, none of which the tables
above predict:

- Kittlitz's Murrelet — silty water at the glacier faces
- Horned Puffin and Tufted Puffin
- Black-legged Kittiwake, in numbers, at colonies
- Common Murre, Marbled Murrelet, Pigeon Guillemot
- Arctic Tern, Glaucous-winged Gull
- Harlequin Duck along the shorelines, Bald Eagle throughout
- Black Oystercatcher on rocky edges

Treat the Glacier Bay day as its own trip. If a park ranger boards for the
day — standard on most itineraries — they are the best local knowledge you will
get all week. Find them early.

## A note on the parsing

The Ketchikan checklist is a rotated, multi-column PDF whose blank cells are
dropped by ordinary text extraction, so its season columns were reconstructed
from glyph coordinates and then validated against the plain-text rows:
254 rows matched with zero mismatches. Juneau and Skagway parsed cleanly from
text. If a code below looks wrong, the source PDFs are in `field-guides/` and
the parent species row is the place to check first.
